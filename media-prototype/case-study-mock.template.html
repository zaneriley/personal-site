Case study · 2023

Helping people find local healthcare

A search experience for finding a nearby doctor who takes your insurance, rebuilt around the questions people actually ask before they book.

Role
Lead product designer
Team
2 designers, 6 engineers
Timeline
9 months
Platforms
Web, iOS, Android

The problem

Most people start looking for a doctor at a bad moment. They have just moved, or their plan changed, or something hurts. The existing flow asked them to pick a specialty, a radius and a network before showing a single result, and many sessions ended on that first form.

Interviews kept returning to the same three questions: will this person take my insurance, can I see them soon, and will they listen to me. The old results page answered the first one in small grey text and ignored the other two.

What we tried

We cut the form to a single field and inferred the rest from location and plan. Early prototypes showed results immediately and let people narrow down afterwards, which tested better than any version of the up-front filter.

“I just want to know if they can see me this week.”

The design system work ran in parallel. The team had five slightly different blues and no shared rules for when to use them, so we wrote the color guidance alongside the product work and shipped both together.

Not everything was screens. Part of the research happened in clinics, and the people we met there shaped the tone of the whole product more than any metric did.

What shipped

The new search launched on web first, then on both apps a quarter later. The first form disappeared entirely. People could still filter, but they did it after seeing real options, and fewer people gave up on the first screen.

A smaller follow-up brought the same pattern to the connected-home settings, where people faced the same wall of choices before seeing anything useful.

Photos: grown edge from the image's own pixels, graded, or tinted at 70%. Screens: a generative field colored from the screenshot, graded and narrowed by default. The sample images are low-resolution crops, so some look soft at this size.
